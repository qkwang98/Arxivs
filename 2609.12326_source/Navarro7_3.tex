	\author[Christopher Herbig]{Christopher Herbig}
	\address{%
		Little Priest Tribal College\\
		Winnebago, NE 68071 \\
		USA}
	\email{christopher.herbig@littlepriest.edu}
	
	
	
	\subjclass[2020]{Primary 20C15, 11R18, 20D10}
	
	\keywords{characters, finite groups, character values, solvable groups}
	
	
	\date{\today}
	
	\maketitle
	
	\begin{abstract}
		In a 2023 survey paper, Gabriel Navarro posed the following problem: Given an irreducible character $\chi$ of some solvable group $G$ where $\chi$ either is 2-rational or has odd degree, does there exist some $g \in G$ such that $\Q(\chi(g)) = \Q(\chi)$? When $\chi$ is imprimitive, the answer is no in general. In the case where $\chi$ is primitive, we are able to show that there exists $g \in G$ such that $\Gal(\Q(\chi)/\Q(\chi(g)))$ is an elementary abelian 2-group. We are then able to show that the answer to Navarro's problem is yes when we further assume that $c(\chi)$ is divisible by at most two primes.
	\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

We first establish some notation and terminology. All groups are assumed to be finite, and all class functions of any group are assumed to take values in $\C$. Given a group $G$ and a class function $\chi$, we define $\Q(\chi)$ to be the field extension of $\Q$ generated by all values of $\chi$. We will use $\zeta_n$ to denote the primitive $n$th root of unity $\exp(2\pi i / n)$, and $\Q_n$ will denote the extension of $\Q$ generated by $\zeta_n$. Given any cyclotomic integer $\alpha$, we define $c(\alpha)$ to be the unique smallest integer such that $\Q(\alpha) \subseteq \Q_{c(\alpha)}$. We call $c(\alpha)$ the \emph{conductor} of $\alpha$. The conductor of a class function $\chi$, denoted $c(\chi)$, is defined analogously. If $n$ is an integer and $\pi$ is a set of primes, then $n_{\pi}$ is taken to be the largest divisor of $n$ whose prime divisors all lie in $\pi$. If $\pi = \{p\}$ for some prime $p$, then we will simply write $n_p$ instead of $n_{\{p\}}$. Given a set of primes $\pi$, we let $\pi'$ denote complement of $\pi$ among the set of all primes. Likewise, if $\pi = \{p\}$ for some prime $p$, we will simply write $p'$ nstead of $\{p\}'$. For a group $G$, we let $\pi(G)$ denote the set of prime divisors of $|G|$. Otherwise, our notation will follow that of Isaacs in \cite{Is}. 

Gabriel Navarro has posed the following in \cite[Problem 7.3]{Nav}: If $G$ is a solvable group and $\chi \in \Irr(G)$ either is 2-rational or has odd degree, does there exist $g \in G$ such that $\Q(\chi(g)) = \Q(\chi)$? Using artificial intelligence, Ulrich Thiel has shown the answer to be no in \cite{Thiel} even when $G$ is assumed to have odd order. However, each of the solvable counterexamples he provides are in fact supersolvable groups, whose nonlinear characters are never primitive since such groups are $M$-groups. We wonder whether the answer is yes when $\chi$ is merely assumed to be primitive. We note that by Berger's Theorem (cf. \cite[Theorem 11.33]{Is}), there is no distinction in solvable groups between quasiprimitive and primitive characters, so we will not distinguish between the two classes of characters for the remainder of this work.
\begin{conjecture}\label{big-conj}
	Let $G$ be a solvable group, and let $\chi \in \Irr(G)$ be a primitive character which either is 2-rational or has odd degree. There exists $g \in G$ such that $\Q(\chi(g)) = \Q(\chi)$. 
\end{conjecture}

We are unable to prove this conjecture or find counterexamples at the moment, and the aim of this work is to present partial results in this direction.  To state our main results, we  require the notion of a ``magic field automorphism'' of $\Gal(\Q_n)$, which Isaacs defines in Section 2B of \cite{Is2} as a certain type of automorphism of $\Q_n$ for an odd integer $n$. For our purposes though, it will be  convenient to extend this definition to even integers. First, recall that for an integer $n$, we may write $\Q_n = \Q_{n_\pi}\Q_{n_{\pi'}}$ where $\Q_{n_\pi} \cap \Q_{n_{\pi'}} = \Q$. Thus, defining a Galois automorphism on $\Q_{n_\pi}$ and $\Q_{n_{\pi'}}$ amounts to defining a Galois automorphism on $\Q_n$.
\begin{definition}
	Let $n$ be an integer, and let $\pi$ be a set of primes. We may write $\Q_n = \Q_{n_\pi}\Q_{n_{\pi'}}$. A \emph{magic field automorphism} $\tau_\pi \in \Gal(\Q_n)$ with respect to $\pi$ is the automorphism defined by letting $\tau_\pi$ act as the trivial automorphism on $\Q_{n_{\pi'}}$ and as complex conjugation on $\Q_{n_{\pi - \{2\}}}$. Further, if $2 \in \pi$ and $4 \mid n$, then $\tau_\pi$ acts on $\Q_{n_2}$ by sending $\zeta_{n_2}$ to $-\zeta_{n_2}$. 
\end{definition} 

If $n$ is odd or divisible by 4, then it is clear that the subgroup of magic field automorphisms in $\Gal(\Q_n)$ is an elementary abelian 2-group whose rank is the number of prime divisors of $n$.

For a group $G$, recall that $\chi \in \Irr(G)$ is said to be fully factorizable if $\chi$ is expressible as a product of $p$-special characters $\chi_p \in \Irr(G)$ for primes $p \in \pi$, and recall also that primitive characters of solvable groups are fully factorizable (cf. \cite[Theorem 2.9]{Is2})

\begin{thmA}
	Let $G$ be a solvable group, and let $\chi \in \Irr(G)$ be fully factorizable. Further, assume either  that $\chi(1)$ is odd or that $\chi$ is 2-rational. There exists $g \in G$ such that $\Gal(\Q(\chi)/\Q(\chi(g)))$ either is trivial or consists of magic field automorphisms $\tau_\pi$ such that $|\pi|$ is even. In particular, $\Gal(\Q(\chi)/\Q(\chi(g)))$ is an elementary abelian 2-group of rank strictly less than $\pi(G)$.
\end{thmA}
When we further assume that $\chi$ is primitive and that $c(\chi)$ is divisible by at most two primes, the answer to Navarro's question is yes.
\begin{thmB}
	Let $G$ be a solvable group, and let $\chi \in \Irr(G)$ be a primitive character such that $\chi(1)$ is odd or that $\chi$ is 2-rational. Further assume $c(\chi)$ is divisible by at most two distinct primes. There exists $g \in G$ such that $\Q(\chi) = \Q(\chi(g))$.
\end{thmB}

A benefit of restricting our attention to primitive characters is that we have access to Gajendragadkar's theory of $p$-special characters. This theory will play a crucial role in our arguments. In particular, our proof for Theorem A is essentially modeled after the proof of Feit's Conjecture provided in \cite[Theorem 2.22]{Is2}. We state Feit's conjecture \cite{F80} for reference:

\begin{conjecture}
	Let $G$ be a group, and let $\chi \in \Irr(G)$. There exists $g \in G$ having order $c(\chi)$.
\end{conjecture}

The connection between Navarro's problem and the Feit conjecture is not coincidental. Specifically, whenever the conclusion of Conjecture \ref{big-conj} holds for an irreducible character $\chi$ of some group $G$, then Feit's conjecture will hold for that character as a result. To see this, assume there exists $g \in G$ such that $\Q(\chi(g)) = \Q(\chi)$. Clearly, $c(\chi(g)) = c(\chi)$. Now, if $\mathfrak{X}$ is a representation affording $\chi$, then the order of $g$ is the least common multiple of the orders of the eigenvalues of $\mathfrak{X}(g)$. Since $\chi(g)$ lies in the field generated by these eigenvalues, it follows that $\Q_{c(\chi)} = \Q_{c(\chi(g))} \subseteq \Q_{o(g)}$. We conclude that $c(\chi)$ divides the order of $g$, and the claim follows.

This work is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review some basic facts about cyclotomic fields and Gajendragadkar's theory, and we will prove some number-theoretic lemmas which will prove useful throughout. In Section 3, we prove that the conclusion of Conjecture \ref{big-conj} holds for $p$-special characters whenever $p$ is an odd prime. Lastly, in Section 4, we prove Theorems A and B. 

\section{Preliminary Lemmas}

Given a prime $p$ and two integers $a$ and $b$ where $a < b$, we have the following containment: $\Q_{p^a}\subseteq \Q_{p^b}$. In particular, the cyclotomic fields of the form $\Q_{p^a}$ are linearly ordered by containment. Consequently, given a character $\chi$ of a group $G$ having a prime-power conductor, there exists an element $g \in G$ such that the conductor of $\chi(g)$ equals the conductor of $\chi$ itself. We state this as a lemma for future use.
\begin{lemma}\label{L:2:1}
	Let $\chi$ be a character of a group $G$, and assume $c(\chi) = p^a$ for some prime $p$ an integer $a$. There exists $g \in G$ such that $c(\chi(g)) = c(\chi)$.
\end{lemma}

We will now review some basic facts about $p$-special characters. Proofs for each of the stated facts can be found in Chapter 2 of \cite{Is2}. 

Let $\chi$ be a primitive character of a solvable group $G$, and let $\pi := \pi(G)$ be the set of prime divisors of $|G|$. For each $p \in \pi(G)$, we are able to factorize $\chi$ like so:
$$
\chi = \prod_{p \in \pi(G)} \chi_p
$$
where each $\chi_p \in \Irr(G)$ is $p$-special. The $\chi_p$ satisfy many nice properties. For each $p$, we have that $c(\chi_p) = p^a$ for some $a$. By Gajendragadkar's restriction theorem, the restriction of $\chi_p$ to any Sylow $p$-subgroup is irreducible. The above factorization of $\chi$ is unique up to the order of the factors. The field of values of $\chi$ can also be nicely expressed in terms of the fields generated by its $p$-special factors. The statement in the lemma below is taken from Lemma 2.19 in \cite{Is2}, and we state this result here since we shall refer to them frequently:
\begin{lemma}\label{L:Is-stuff}
	Let $G$ be a group, and let $\chi \in \Irr(G)$ be fully factorizable with factorization
		$$
		\chi = \prod_{p} \chi_p,
		$$
		where $\chi_p \in \Irr(G)$ is $p$-special. We have
		$$
		\Q(\chi) = \Q(\{\chi_p(g) \; | \; g \in G, \; p \in \pi(G)\}).
		$$
\end{lemma}

We are now able to prove the following proposition.

\begin{proposition}\label{P:2:1}
	Let $\psi \in \Irr(G)$ be $p$-special for an odd prime $p$. Then, $\Q(\psi) = \Q_{c(\psi)}$. In particular, if $\chi$ is a primitive character of a solvable group and $\chi$ either has odd degree or is 2-rational, then $\Q(\chi) = \Q_{c(\chi)}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
	Let $\psi \in \Irr(G)$ be $p$-special for an odd prime $p$. By replacing $G$ with $G/\ker\psi$, it is of no loss to assume that $\psi$ is faithful. We have that $c(\psi)$ is an integer power of $p$, say $p^a$. Of course, if $\psi$ is rational valued, there is nothing to prove, so we may assume otherwise. 
	
	Now, $|\Q_{c(\psi)} : \Q| = (p-1)p^{a-1}$. There exists $g \in G$ such that $p^{a-1}$ divides $|\Q(\chi(g)) : \Q|$by Lemma \ref{L:2:1}. On the other hand, let $P$ be a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $G$. Since $\psi_P$ is irreducible, we have $\Cent(P) \subseteq \Cent(\psi)$. Thus, there exists $h \in G$ such that $\psi(h) = \zeta_p\psi(1)$. Now, one verifies that $\Q(\psi) \supseteq \Q(\psi(g),\psi(h)) = \Q_{c(\psi)}$, and so $\Q(\psi) = \Q_{c(\psi)}$.
	
	For the second claim, let $\chi$ be a primitive character of $G$, and assume that $\chi(1)$ is odd or that $\chi$ is 2-rational. We may factorize $\chi$ as a product of $p$-special characters:
	$$
		\chi = \prod_{p \in \pi(G)} \chi_p.
	$$
	By Lemma \ref{L:Is-stuff}, if $\chi$ is 2-rational, then $\chi_2$ is rational valued. Otherwise, if $\chi$ has odd degree, then $\chi_2$ is linear. In either case, $\Q(\chi_2) = \Q_{2^a}$ for some nonnegative integer $a$. In particular, the values of any $\chi_p$ generate a full cyclotomic field, and the result holds by Lemma \ref{L:Is-stuff}.
\end{proof}

We remark that the first statement in the above proposition fails to hold when $p = 2$. Taking the quaternion group of order 16 for instance, the two nonlinear, irrational characters each generate the subfield of $\Q_8$ fixed by complex conjugation. Also, for any irreducible character $\chi$ of an arbitrary solvable group, Cram has given a very short proof in \cite{Cram} that $|\Q_{c(\chi)} : \Q(\chi)|$ divides $\chi(1)$. For an irreducible character $\chi$ of a general finite group, it was conjectured in \cite{HT} that $|\Q_{c(\chi)} : \Q(\chi)| \leq \chi(1)$, and this conjecture remains open.

For the lemma below, we introduce more notation and recall some basic facts about the Galois groups of cyclotomic fields. Let $n$ be an integer with prime factorization $n = \prod_{p \mid n} p^{a_p}$ for $a_p \geq 1$. The Galois group $\Gal(\Q_n)$ decomposes as a direct product: $$\Gal(\Q_n) = \prod_{p \mid n} \Gal(\Q_{p^{a_p}}).$$ Specifically, given $\sigma \in \Gal(\Q_n)$, we may uniquely write $\sigma= \prod_{p \mid n}\sigma_p$, where $\sigma_p$ fixes $\Q_{q^{a_q}}$ for $q \neq p$.

\begin{lemma}\label{L:2:3}
	Let $n$ be an integer with prime factorization $n = \prod_{p \mid n} p^{a_p}$ for $a_p \geq 1$, and for each $p$ dividing $n$, let $\alpha_p \in \Q_{p^{a_p}}$.
	Further assume that if $n$ is even, then $\alpha_2$ is a root of unity. Set $\alpha = \prod_{p \mid n} \alpha_p$, and assume there exists a nonidentity Galois automorphism $\sigma \in \Gal(\Q_n/\Q(\alpha))$. Either of the following occur:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item[(i).] $\alpha$ is fixed by some $\sigma_p$. Further, if $n$ is even and $\alpha$ is fixed by $\sigma_2$, then we may assume $\sigma_2$ is the automorphism of $\Gal(\Q_{2^{a_2}})$ sending $\zeta_{2^{a_2}}$ to $-
		\zeta_{2^{a_2}}$.
		\item[(ii).] $\sigma$ is a magic field automorphism with respect to some set $\pi$. Here, $|\pi|$ is even, and for each $p \in \pi$, we have $\alpha_p^{\tau_\pi} = -\alpha_p$. 
	\end{itemize}

\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	Keeping the above notation, if $\alpha_p$ is fixed by any nontrivial Galois automorphism of $\Q_{p^{a_p}}$, say $\tau$, then $\tau$ extends to an automorphism of $\Q_n$ which leaves $\Q_{q^{a_q}}$ fixed for $q \neq p$. Also, notice that in the case where $p = 2$ here, since $\alpha_2$ is a root of unity, the fact that $\tau$ fixes $\alpha_2$ implies that $c(\alpha_2) < 2^{a_2}$, meaning that the Galois automorphism of $\Gal(\Q_{2^{a_2}})$ sending $\zeta_{2^{a_2}}$ to $-
	\zeta_{2^{a_2}}$ must fix $\alpha_2$. Thus, $\tau$ fixes $\alpha$. Thus, we can assume that $\Q(\alpha_p) = \Q_{p^{a_p}}$ for each $p$ dividing $n$. 
	
	If $\sigma \in \Gal(\Q_n/\Q(\alpha))$ is a nonidentity automorphism, we have
	$$
	\prod_{p \mid n} \alpha_p = \alpha^\sigma =  \alpha = \prod_{p\mid n} \alpha_p^{\sigma_p} ,
	$$
	and so
	\begin{equation}\label{rat-prod}  
	1 = \prod_{p \mid n} \dfrac{\alpha_p^{\sigma_p}}{\alpha_p}.
	\end{equation} 
	Now, it is straightforward to verify using the
Galois Correspondence that each factor in the above
 product must be rational. Thus, for each $p$,
  there exists a rational number $r_p$ such that $\alpha_p^{\sigma_p} = r_p\alpha_p$.  If
   $\sigma_p$ has order $\ell$, then $\alpha_p = \alpha_p^{\sigma_p^\ell} = r_p^\ell\alpha_p$. We conclude that $r_p$ is a rational
  root of unity and is therefore either 1 or $-1$. 
  
  Now, since we are assuming $\sigma$ is a nonidentity automorphism, at least one $\sigma_p$ is a nonidentity automorphism. If there exists a $p$ for which $\sigma_p$ is nontrivial and $r_p = 1$, then $\alpha$ is fixed by $\sigma_p$. Thus, from now on, we may assume that for each $p$ either $\sigma_p$ is trivial or $r_p = -1$. 
  
 If $p$ is odd, we claim that if $r_p = -1$, then $\sigma_p$ acts as complex conjugation on $\Q_{p^{a_p}}$. Indeed, $\alpha_p^{\sigma_p} = -\alpha_p$, and so $\sigma_p$ acts as an involution on $\Q(\alpha_p) = \Q_{p^{a_p}}$. When $p$ is odd, $\Gal(\Q_{p^{a_p}})$ has one involution, namely complex conjugation. Thus, for each odd $p$ where $r_p = -1$, $\sigma$ acts as complex conjugation on $\Gal(\Q_{p^{a_p}})$, and $\sigma$ acts trivially for all $p$ where $r_p = 1$. We conclude that $\sigma$ is a magic field automorphism with respect to $\pi$ where $\pi$ is the set of all $p$ such that $r_p = -1$. The fact that $|\pi|$ is even now follows from the fact that $r_p$ takes values in $\{-1,1\}$ and the fact that \eqref{rat-prod} becomes
 $$
 1 = \prod_{p \mid n} \dfrac{\alpha_p^{\sigma_p}}{\alpha_p} =  \prod_{p \mid n} \dfrac{r_p\alpha_p}{\alpha_p} = \prod_{p \mid n} r_p.
 $$
\end{proof} 

\section{$p$-special Characters}

We will prove that the conclusion of Conjecture \ref{big-conj} holds when $\chi$ is assumed to be $p$-special. This fact will follow somewhat readily from the below proposition. If $p$ is an odd prime and $a$ is an integer, recall that $\Gal(\Q_{p^a})$ is cyclic and therefore has a unique subgroup of order $k$ for each divisor $k$ of $\varphi(p^a) = (p-1)p^{a-1}$.

\begin{proposition}
	Let $p$ be an odd prime, and let $\alpha$ be a cyclotomic integer with $c(\alpha) = p^a$ where $a \geq 1$. There exist at least two distinct integers $i, j \in \{0,1,\ldots, p-1\}$ such that $\Q(\zeta_p^i\alpha) = \Q(\zeta_p^j\alpha) = \Q_{p^a}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
	Let $\alpha$ be as above. Also, let $K$ be the unique subfield of $\Q_{p^a}$ of degree $p^{a-1}$ over $\Q$. Since $c(\alpha) = p^{a}$, we have that $|\Q(\alpha) : \Q|_p = p^{a-1}$. In particular, $K \subseteq \Q(\alpha)$. We first wish to show that $K \subseteq  \Q(\zeta_p^i\alpha)$ for each integer $i$. This amounts to showing that $|\Q_{p^a} : \Q(\zeta_p^i\alpha)|$ divides $p-1$ for each integer $i$.
	
	Fix an integer $i$ where $\gcd(i,p) = 1$, and choose a Galois automorphism $\sigma \in \Gal(\Q_{p^a}/\Q(\zeta_p^i\alpha))$. We may write
	$$
	\zeta_p^i \alpha = (\zeta_p\alpha)^\sigma = (\zeta_p^i)^\sigma\alpha^\sigma.
	$$
	Equivalently,
	$$
	\alpha^{\sigma} = (\zeta_p^i)^{1-\sigma}\alpha.
	$$
	By iteratively, applying $\sigma$ to $\alpha$, we obtain
	$$
	\alpha^{\sigma^{p-1}} = (\zeta_p^i)^{1-\sigma^{p-1}}\alpha.
	$$
	The restriction of $\sigma$ to $\Q_p$ has order dividing $p-1$, and so we have that $\alpha^{\sigma^{p-1}} = \alpha$. Now, $\sigma^{p-1}$ fixes both $\alpha$ and $\zeta_p^i$. However, $\Q(\zeta_p^i,\alpha) = \Q_{p^a}$, so $\sigma^{p-1}$ is the identity automorphism. Since $\sigma$ was arbitrary, we conclude that $ \Gal(\Q_{p^a}/\Q(\zeta_p^i\alpha))$ has order dividing $p-1$, and it follows as desired that $K \subseteq \Q(\zeta_p^i\alpha)$.
	
	Now, we show that the $p$ fields of the form $\Q(\zeta_p^i\alpha)$ for $i=0,1,\ldots, p-1$ are not distinct. We have established that each of these fields lies between $K$ and $\Q_{p^a}$. However, by the Galois Correspondence, the number of fields lying between $K$ and $\Q_{p^a}$ is exactly the number of divisors of $p-1$, which is of course strictly less than $p$. Therefore, there exist integers $i$ and $j$ where $0 \leq i < j \leq p-1$ and where $\Q(\zeta_p^i\alpha) = \Q(\zeta_p^j\alpha)$. We claim that this choice of $i$ and $j$ will satisfy the conditions of the lemma. Writing $L = \Q(\zeta_p^i\alpha) = \Q(\zeta_p^j\alpha)$, it suffices to show that $L = \Q_{p^a}$.
	
	Choose $\tau \in \Gal(\Q_{p^a}/L)$. We have that 
	$$
	\zeta_p^{j-i} = \dfrac{\zeta_p^j\alpha}{\zeta_p^i\alpha} =  \bigg(\dfrac{\zeta_p^j\alpha}{\zeta_p^i\alpha}\bigg)^\tau = (\zeta_p^{j-i})^\tau. 
	$$
In particular, $\tau$ fixes $\zeta^{j-i}$ which is a primitive $p$th root of unity, and $\tau$ therefore fixes all $p$th roots of unity. Now, we also have
$$
\zeta_p^i\alpha = (\zeta_p^i\alpha)^\tau = \zeta_{p}^i\alpha^\tau,
$$
from which it follows that $\tau$ fixes $\alpha$ as well. Since $\tau$ fixes $\zeta_p$ and $\alpha$ and since $\Q(\zeta_p,\alpha) = \Q_{p^a}$, it follows that $\tau$ is the identity automorphism. Since $\tau$ was arbitrary, it follows that $\Gal(\Q_{p^a}/L)$ is trivial, and hence, $L = \Q_{p^a}$ as desired. 
\end{proof}


\begin{corollary}
	Let $G$ be a solvable group, and let $\chi \in \Irr(G)$ be $p$-special for an odd prime $p$. There exists $g \in G$ such that $\Q(\chi) = \Q(\chi(g))$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
	We may of course assume without loss that $\chi$ is faithful. By Proposition \ref{P:2:1}, $\Q(\chi) = \Q_{c(\chi)}$. Let $c(\chi) = p^a$ for some integer $a$. If $a = 0$, then $\chi$ is rational valued, and there is nothing to prove. Thus, we may assume $a \geq 1$. 
	
	By Lemma \ref{L:2:1}, there exists $g \in G$ such that $c(\chi(g)) = c(\chi) = p^a$. By the above lemma, there exists a primitive $p$th root of unity $\varepsilon$ such that $\Q(\varepsilon\chi(g)) = \Q_{c(\chi)}$. It now suffices to show that there exists $h \in G$ such that $\chi(h) = \varepsilon\chi(g)$.
	
	 For $P \in \Syl_p(G)$, since $\chi$ is $p$-special, we have that $\chi_P$ is irreducible. Thus, $\chi_{\Cent(P)} = \chi(1)\lambda$ for some $\lambda \in \Irr(\Cent(P))$, and since $\chi$ is faithful, there exists $z \in \Cent(G)$ such that $\chi(z) = \chi(1)\varepsilon$.  
	 Now, let $\mathfrak{X}$ be a representation affording $\chi$, and observe that $\mathfrak{X}(z) = \varepsilon I$. We have that $\mathfrak{X}(zg) = \mathfrak{X}(z)\mathfrak{X}(g) = \varepsilon\mathfrak{X}(g)$. Thus, $\chi(zg) = \varepsilon\chi(g)$, as desired.
\end{proof}
 

\section{Proofs for the Main Theorems}

Let $n$ be an integer, and let $\sigma \in \Gal(\Q_n)$. We define the function $f_{\sigma}: \Q_n \to \Q_n$ by setting $f_\sigma(\alpha) = \alpha - \alpha^\sigma$. Since the Galois group is abelian, it is straightforward to see that the functions are commutative under composition, i.e., $f_\sigma \circ f_\tau = f_\tau \circ f_\sigma$ for $\sigma, \tau \in \Gal(\Q_n)$. 

Also, let $X \subseteq \Gal(\Q_n)$, and let $f_X$ denote the composition of all $f_\sigma$ for $\sigma \in X$. The commutativity of the $f_\sigma$ under composition implies that $f_X$ is well defined.  As a convention, if $X$ is empty, we take $f_X$ to be the identity automorphism. At a few points, we will require the following basic facts, which we state as a lemma.
\begin{lemma}
	Let $n$ be an integer, and let $X \subseteq \Gal(\Q_n)$. The following hold:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item[(a).] If $\alpha$ lies in a subfield of $\Q_n$ fixed by any element of $X$, then $f_X(\alpha) = 0$.
		\item[(b).] For any $\alpha \in \Q_n$, we have
		$$
		f_X(\alpha) = \sum_{i=0}^{|X|} (-1)^i\sum_{A \in \mathscr{X}_i}\alpha^{\prod_{\sigma \in A}\sigma}, 
		$$
		where $\mathscr{X}_i$ is the set of all size-$i$ subsets of $X$. (Vacuous products are of course taken to be the identity.)
	\end{itemize}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	For (a), if $\sigma \in X$ fixes $\alpha \in \Q_n$, then we may write 
	$$
		f_X(\alpha) = f_{X - \{\sigma\}}(f_\sigma(\alpha) ) = f_{X - \{\sigma\}}(0) = 0,
	$$
	from which the claim follows.
	
	The formula in part (b) follows easily from induction on the cardinality of $X$.
\end{proof} 

We are now ready to prove Theorem A.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem A]
	Let $G$ be solvable, and let $\chi \in \Irr(G)$ be fully factorizable, and assume either that $\chi(1)$ is odd or that $\chi$ is 2-rational. By assumption, $\chi$ is fully factorizable with factorization say,
	$$
		\chi = \prod_{p \in \pi} \chi_{p},
	$$
	for some set of primes $\pi$ and where $\chi_{p}$ is $p$-special for each prime $p \in \pi$. If $|\pi| = 1$, then the result holds by Corollary 3.2 for odd primes $p$, and if $\pi = \{2\}$, then the theorem holds trivially since $\chi$ is either linear or rational valued by our assumptions. Thus, we may assume that $|\pi| \geq 2$. Using induction, we may also assume that the theorem holds for all characters which are fully factorizable over a smaller set of primes. In particular, by Lemma \ref{L:Is-stuff}, we may assume that each $\chi_p$ in the factorization takes irrational values. 
	
	By Proposition \ref{P:2:1}, we have that $\Q(\chi) = \Q_n$ for some integer $n$ having prime factorization $n = \prod_{p \in \pi} p^{a_p}$ where $a_p \geq 1$ for each $p$. In particular, for any two distinct $\sigma, \tau \in \Gal(\Q_n)$, we have that $\chi^{\sigma} \neq \chi^\tau$.
	
	Now, $\Gal(\Q_n)$ decomposes as a direct product like so:
	$$
		\Gal(\Q_n) = \prod_{p \in \pi} \Gal(\Q_{p^{a_p}}).
	$$
	For odd primes $p$, we have $\Gal(\Q_{p^{a_p}}) \cong C_{p-1} \times C_{p^{a_p - 1}}$. For each odd prime $p \in \pi$ and each prime divisor $q$ of $\varphi(p^{a_p}) = (p-1)p^{a_p-1}$ choose an element $\sigma_{p,q}$ of order $q$ from $\Gal(\Q_{p^{a_p}})$. If $4 \mid n$, let $\sigma_2$ denote the automorphism of $\Q_{2^{a_2}}$ sending $\zeta_{2^{a_2}}$ to $-\zeta_{2^{a_2}}$ Let $X$ denote the set consisting of $\sigma_2$ (if $4 \mid n$) and all $\sigma_{p,q}$ for each odd $p \in \pi$ and $q$ dividing $(p-1)p^{a_p - 1}$. Since $\sigma_2$ and each $\sigma_{p,q}$ lie in a unique direct factor of $\Gal(\Q_n)$, it follows that all automorphisms of the form
	$$
		\prod_{\sigma \in A} \sigma 
	$$
	for $A \subseteq X$ are distinct (Again, vacuous products are taken to be the identity). Now, defining $f_X$ to be the composition of all $f_\sigma$ for $\sigma \in X$, it follows from the lemma above that
	$$
	f_X(\chi) = \sum_{i=0}^{|X|} (-1)^i\sum_{A \in \mathscr{X}_i}\chi^{\prod_{\sigma \in A}\sigma}.
	$$
	Each term in this sum is a distinct irreducible character and the generalized character is therefore nonzero. 
	
	By Lemma \ref{L:2:3}, if $\chi(g)$ is fixed by some 
	Galois automorphism which is not a magic field automorphism for some $g \in G$, then $\chi(g)$ is 
fixed by some Galois automorphism of the form $\sigma_p$ for $p \in \pi$ which leaves $\Q_{q^{a_q}}$ 
	fixed for all $q \neq p$. In particular, $\chi(g)$ is fixed by some element of $X$, and we conclude that
$f_X(\chi(g)) = 0$. Thus, $f_X(\chi)$ vanishes on all elements of $G$ except for the $g \in G$ where $\Gal(\Q_n/\Q(\chi(g)))$ 
consists of magic field automorphisms with respect to sets having even cardinality. Now, the set of all $\tau_\pi$ where $\pi$ is even is an elementary abelian group of rank $|\pi| - 1$, and the theorem follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem B]
	Let $\chi$ be a primitive character of a solvable group $G$, and assume that $c(\chi)$ is divisible by at most 2 primes. If $c(\chi)$ is a prime power, then the theorem is a consequence of Corollary 3.2. Thus, we may assume that $c(\chi) = p^aq^b$ for distinct primes $p$ and $q$ and for positive integers $a$ and $b$. Now, $\chi$ has factorization
	$$
	\chi_p\chi_q\prod_{i=1}^k \chi_{p_i}
	$$
	where $\chi_p$ is $p$-special, $\chi_q$ is $q$-special, and the $\chi_{p_i}$ are $p_i$-special and rational valued for $i=1,\ldots,k$. By Lemma \ref{L:Is-stuff}, it suffices to show that the claim holds for $\chi_p\chi_q$, which we will for now denote as $\psi$, and in particular, if either $p$ or $q$ is 2, then respectively $\chi_p$ or $\chi_q$ is linear. Notice that since $\chi$ is primitive, then so is $\psi$ by Exercise 5.3 of \cite{Is}. Letting $H$ be a Hall $\{p,q\}$-subgroup of $G$, it follows from Corollary 2.25 of \cite{Is2} that $\Q(\psi) = \Q(\psi_H)$. Since $\psi_H$ is irreducible and fully factorizable by Gajendragadkar's restriction theorem, it is of no loss to replace $G$ with $H$ and $\chi$ with $\psi_H$, except that $\chi$ may no longer be primitive. Also, it is clearly of no loss to assume that $\chi$ is faithful.
	
	Assume for a contradiction that there exists no $g \in G$ such that $\Q(\chi(g)) = \Q(\chi)$. By Theorem A, there exists $g \in G$ such that $\Gal(\Q(\chi)/\Q(\chi(g)))$ is a nontrivial group consisting of magic field automorphisms. Let $\sigma$ be a nonidentity element of this group. $\sigma$ is a magic field automorphism with respect to some set of primes $\pi$ where $\pi$ is even. However, as $|\pi(G)| = 2$, it follows that $\pi = \pi(G)$, $|\Gal(\Q(\chi)/\Q(\chi(g)))| = 2$, and $\sigma$ is precisely the automorphism of complex conjugation. Also, we have that $\chi_p(g)^\sigma = -\chi_p(g)$ and $\chi_q(g)^\sigma = -\chi_q(g)$.
	
	Now, since $G$ is solvable, either $O_p(G)$ or $O_q(G)$ is nontrivial, so we will assume that $O_p(G)$ is nontrivial. We claim that we can also assume that $p$ is odd. Otherwise, if $p = 2$ and $O_q(G)$ is trivial, then since $\chi$ is assumed to have odd degree, we have that $\chi_p$ is linear, and since the restriction of $\chi_p$ to a Sylow $p$-subgroup is necessarily faithful, it follows that the Sylow $2$-subgroups of $G$ are cyclic. We conclude that $G'$ is a $q$-subgroup, contradicting our assumption that $O_q(G) = 1$.
	
	Assume $p$ is odd and $N:= O_p(G)$ is nontrivial. We have that $N \subseteq \ker(\chi_q)$, and the restriction of $\chi_p$ to $\Cent(N)$ is of the form $\chi_p(1)\lambda$ for some nontrivial $\lambda \in \Irr(\Cent(N))$. We conclude that $\chi_{\Cent(N)} = \chi(1)\lambda$. Since $\chi$ is faithful, there exists $z \in \Cent(N)$ such that $\chi(z) = \chi(1)\zeta_p$. We have that $\chi(z^ig) = \zeta_p^i\chi(g) = \zeta_p^i\chi_{p}(g)\chi_q(g)$. By Proposition 3.1, there is at least one integer $k$ where $\gcd(k,p) = 1$ and where $\zeta_p^k\chi_p(g)$ generates a full cyclotomic field. Since $p$ is odd and $\ol{\chi(g)} = \chi(g)$, $\chi(z^kg)$ is not fixed conjugation. Also, both $\zeta_p^k\chi_p(g)$ and $\chi_q(g)$ generate full cyclotomic fields. We conclude by Lemma \ref{L:2:3} that $\Q(\chi(z^kg)) = \Q_n$. This proves the theorem.
\end{proof}

\section{Concluding Remarks}

A sort of intermediate question to Navarro's problem and the Feit conjecture is the following question, which was first considered in \cite{HH}:

\begin{question}
	Let $G$ be a group, and let $\chi \in \Irr(G)$. When does there exist $g \in G$ such that $c(\chi(g)) = c(\chi)$.
\end{question}

It is straightforward to verify that for any $\chi$ where the conclusion of Conjecture \ref{big-conj} holds, then the answer to this question is yes. Likewise, whenever the answer to this question is yes for some $\chi$, then Feit's conjecture holds for $\chi$. The answer is known to be no in general for primitive characters of solvable groups. In particular, \texttt{SmallGroup(960,730)} in GAP \cite{Gap} is a supersolvable group possessing an irreducible character for which the answer is no. However, the answer is yes for primitive characters of solvable groups. In fact, the proof for the solvable case of the Feit conjecture provided in \cite[Theorem 2.22]{Is2} generalizes readily to this more general question when $\chi$ is assumed to be primitive (or even just fully factorizable):

\begin{theorem}
	Let $G$ be solvable, and let $\chi \in \Irr(G)$ be fully factorizable as a product of $p$-special characters. There exists $g \in G$ such that $c(\chi(g)) = c(\chi)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	Let $\chi$ be as above and set $n = c(\chi)$. Let $\chi$ have factorization
	$$
	\chi = \prod_p \chi_p 
	$$
	where each $\chi_p$ is $p$-special.
	By Lemma \ref{L:Is-stuff}, we have that $c(\chi_p) = n_p$ for each $p$. Thus, for any prime divisor of $q$ not equal to $p$, we have that $\Q(\chi_p) \subseteq \Q_{n/q}$.
	
	Letting $q$ be a prime divisor of $n$, and notice that $\Q_{n/q} \subset \Q(\zeta_{n/q}, \chi) \subseteq \Q_n$. We may choose a Galois automorphism $\tau_q \in \Gal(\Q_n/\Q_{n/q})$ which does not leave $\Q(\zeta_{n/q}, \chi)$ fixed. In particular, $\tau_q$ acts nontrivially on the values of $\chi$, and $\chi^{\tau_q} \neq \chi$.
	
	Now, for distinct primes $p$ and $q$ dividing $n$, we have that $\tau_q$ fixes $\chi_p$ since $\Q(\chi_p) \subseteq \Q_{n/q}$. Thus, since the factorization of $\chi$ into $p$-special characters is unique up to the order of the factors, we conclude that $\chi_q^{\tau_q} \neq \chi_q$.  
	
	If we assume that there exists no $g \in G$ such that $c(\chi(g)) = n$, then for every $x \in G$,  $c(\chi(x))$ properly divides $n$, and there exists a prime $q$ such that $c(\chi(x))$ divides $n/q$. For such an $x$, it follows that for each $p$, we have $\chi_p(x) \subseteq \Q_{n/q}$, and in particular, $\chi_q(x) \in \Q_{n/q}$. We conclude that $\chi_q(x)^{\tau_q} = \chi_q(x)$.	Thus, the below product is identically zero:
	$$
	0= \prod_q (\chi_q - \chi_p^{\tau_p}).
	$$
	Let $\pi(n)$ denote the set of prime divisors of $n.$ We may rewrite this product as
	$$
	0= \sum_{A \subseteq \pi(n)} (-1)^{|A|}\prod_{q} \chi_q^{\sigma_q}
	$$
	where $\sigma_q = \tau_q$ if $q \in A$ and $\sigma_q = 1$ otherwise. Now, one notices that each product of $p$-special characters in the above sum is a distinct irreducible character since fully factorizable characters factor uniquely. Thus, the sum cannot be zero by the linear independence of irreducible characters, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

The authors in \cite{HH} also showed that the answer is yes for the groups $\GL(2,q)$ where $q$ is a prime power, and they also proved that the conclusion of Conjecture \ref{big-conj} holds for $\SL(2,q)$ and the Suzuki 2-groups.
