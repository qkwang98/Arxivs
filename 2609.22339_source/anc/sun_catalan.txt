Abstract.
Whether the constant
 $G=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(2k+1)^{2}}=\frac{1}{1^{2}}-\frac{1}{3^{2}}+\frac{1}{5^{2}}-\frac{1}{7^{2}}+\cdots$ 
introduced by Catalan in the nineteen century is irrational, is a long-standing open problem.
In this paper we prove the irrationality of  $G$  via using suitable weights.
Key words and phrases: 
Catalan’s constant, irrationality, determinant.

1. 
Introduction and proof architecture
For a nontrivial Dirichlet character  $\chi$ , the Dirichlet  $L$ -function
is given by
 $L(s,\chi)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^{s}}\ (s=1,2,3,\ldots).$ 
For any integer  $d\equiv 0,1\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 4)$ , we let  $\chi_{d}(n)$  (with  $n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}$ )
be the Kronecker symbol  $(\frac{d}{n})$ . The Dirichlet beta function is defined by
 $\beta(s)=L(s,\chi_{-4})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(2k+1)^{s}}\ \ (s=1,2,3,\ldots).$ 
Note that  $\beta(1)=\pi/4$  is a well-known result due to Leibniz. The constant
given by
 $G=\beta(2)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(2k+1)^{2}}=\frac{1}{1^{2}}-\frac{1}{3^{2}}+\frac{1}{5^{2}}-\frac{1}{7^{2}}+\cdots=0.915965594177219015\cdots.$ 
is named in honor of E. C. Catalan (1814-1894), who first gave an equivalent series and expressions in terms of integrals.
A long-standing unsolved problem is whether Catalan’s constant  $G$  is irrational.
In contrast,  $\beta(1)=\pi/4$  and
 $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^{2}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k)^{2}}=\frac{3}{4}\zeta(2)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}$ 
are irrational since  $\pi$  is transcendental, where  $\zeta$  is the Riemann zeta function.
For two  $q$ -analogues of the identity
 $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2k+1)^{-2}=\pi^{2}/8$ 
(which is equivalent to Euler’s formula  $\zeta(2)=\pi^{2}/6$ ), see Sun 
[
11
]
.
For the constant
 $K=L(2,\chi_{-3})=\sum_{k=9}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{(3k+1)^{2}}-\frac{1}{(3k+2)^{2}}\right),$ 
in 2010 the author (cf. 
[
9
]
) conjectured that
 $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(15k-4)(-27)^{k-1}}{k^{3}\binom{2k}{k}^{2}\binom{3k}{k}}=K\ \text{and}\ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(5k-1)(-144)^{k}}{k^{3}\binom{2k}{k}^{2}\binom{4k}{2k}}=-\frac{45}{2}K$ 
with the first one and the second one later confirmed by Kh. Hessami Pilehrood
and T. Hessami Pilehrood 
[

The author

 $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(4k-1)3^{k}}{(2k-1)k^{2}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{3k}{k}}=2K\ \text{and}\ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{10k-3}{(2k-1)k^{2}3^{k}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{3k}{k}}=\frac{K}{2},$ 
The converging rate of the last series is  $1/81$ . As pointed out by J. Zuniga, the last identity
provides the fastest algorithm to compute the important constant  $K$ . In June 2025,
Lorenz Milla used the formula to compute the first  $10^{12}$  decimal digits of  $K$ .
In 2024, F. Calegari, V. Dimitrov, and Y. Tang

They also provided an adelic viewpoint of  $G$  in the arithmetic-holonomy work,
but they were unable to prove that  $G\not\in\mathbb{Q}$ .

Our sophisticated proof of Theorem 
1.1
 has five main stages.
(1)
The Catalan recurrence is encoded in a weighted finite-difference residual matrix. A polynomial defect argument proves full column rank.
(2)
Three binomial Newton columns complete a selected residual minor to one square determinant. This produces the fixed scalar  $\widehat{q}_{B}$  and its exact minimal integerizer  $H_{B}^{\min}$ .
(3)
The residual determinant is expanded by Cauchy–Binet. The Pascal alternant gives one Vandermonde and the Cauchy determinant gives a second.
(4)
For every odd prime power, a local saturation theorem compares the denominator layer with a test residual layer. This converts all local lower bounds into a legal upper bound for the positive-part height of the same scalar.
(5)
A complete asymptotic ledger is evaluated at  $S/B=1/20$ . The corrected odd-prime small-scale constant, the exact middle-prime integral, and the large-prime gain produce a strict margin.
